\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Modeling the game engine.}
Our goal is to move from modeling one game to modeling a space of
games organized by dynamics. The present results support the first
half of that goal: a rule-conditioned, iterated local-update model is
a strong forward model for PuzzleScript-style grid dynamics and is
more parameter-efficient than several non-iterative baselines. A full
engine-model claim additionally requires smooth scaling with the
number of games, held-out rule-system generalization, meaningful
symbolic decoding, and inverse fitting from transitions.

The broad-corpus and joint-AE results
(Appendices~\ref{app:scaling-curve}, \ref{app:autoencoder},
\ref{app:inverse-fit}) take partial steps in each direction:
training on 94 deduped human games delivers median 1-step
teacher-forced cell-error of $0.15\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}); the joint decoder objective tightens
held-out games' nearest-training-neighbour distance in slot space;
inverse-fitting recovers OOD games' dynamics through the latent
variable to $\sim 10^{-3}$ BCE; and autoregressive sampling from the
empirical slot prior at the 94-game scale clears the engine
validator at $15/25$ random draws and at $9/9$ slot-space
interpolation points. Slot-space interpolation between two training
games stays at $9/9$ engine-loadable when scaled to 199 games,
suggesting the latent line carries enough structure for the decoder
to track; random sampling under the same checkpoint drops to
$4/25$, consistent with the empirical slot prior becoming a poorer
match to the manifold as more games crowd it. The remaining gap in
the engine-modelling story is breaking the multi-grid synthetic-data
ceiling described below.

\paragraph{Toward latent world coders.}
Inverse-fitting a continuous game latent from transitions and decoding
it back to source code suggests a ``latent world coder'' direction:
use a learned dynamics manifold as the search space, then project
successful latent hypotheses into executable symbolic programs. This
is complementary to program-synthesis world-model agents such as
WorldCoder and TheoryCoder, which synthesize Python transition models
from interaction for planning~\cite{tang2024worldcoder,ahmed2025theorycoder},
and to DreamCoder-style systems that grow reusable symbolic libraries
for program induction~\cite{ellis2021dreamcoder}. Our current decoder
does not yet reliably emit engine-loadable programs from optimized
slots, but a mature version would make PuzzleScript-style engines a
natural bridge between neural world models and symbolic program
learning. Interactive world-model benchmarks such as WorldTest /
AutumnBench~\cite{warrier2025benchmarking} and
ARC-AGI-3~\cite{arcprize2026arcagi3} are natural testbeds for such
agents, because acting in novel rule systems after limited interaction with the environment can benefit from the inference of a fast forward model, enabling the use of e.g. search and RL to generate agent policies.

\paragraph{What the iterated local update buys.}
The architecture comparison in \S\ref{sec:results-baselines}
isolates one question: holding the conditioner constant, does the
NCA's iterated-local-update body outperform a non-iterative body?
Our findings suggest the answer is yes on tasks where the engine's
\texttt{again} loop produces non-trivial cascade chains, and roughly
a wash on tasks where one tick of dynamics resolves in a single rule
firing. The NCA's parameter efficiency is also striking: shared
weights with $T \cdot n_\ell$ effective depth use the same parameters
as a one-step model.

\paragraph{Memorization as a confound.}
A key observation in \S\ref{app:ceiling} is that the
single-level / single-grid setting allows the model to learn a
per-state lookup table via cross-attention over rich slot
representations. ``Pool features substitute for depth'' is the
single-level reading; ``the model memorizes per-state outcomes'' is
the multi-level reading of the same architectural configuration.
Both are real, and disentangling them required the multi-grid
synthetic-data regime that we describe in \S\ref{app:ceiling}.
We recommend the multi-grid synth setup as a default check for
future PuzzleScript world-model work: if your model only succeeds at
the single-grid setting, you may not be learning the dynamics.

\paragraph{Open: escaping the multi-grid ceiling.}
Our experiments characterize the ceiling but do not break it. The
remaining candidate fixes that have not been ruled out are
(a) hard / sparse rule-slot routing so the model cannot smear
``fire-once'' across all slots, (b) per-cell halt mechanism so cells
in their fixed point stop updating, (c) curriculum or replay-buffer
training that selectively exposes the model to slide-distance
$\geq 2$ instances, and (d) auxiliary ``is-changing'' mask head
factoring ``where to fire'' from ``what to write.'' A successful
break here would close the most important remaining gap between
authored-data and synthetic-multi-grid performance.

``Near-perfect fidelity'' refers to the on-distribution regime:
training on authored levels of human-written games and evaluating on
the same authored levels gives a median
1-step teacher-forced cell-error of $0.15\%$ on 94 broad-corpus
games (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}). The
multi-grid synthetic ceiling is a separate distribution-shift result:
when the model is trained on synthetic levels of multiple grid
sizes \emph{without} access to authored-data memorisation, current
recipes plateau well above the on-distribution regime. The two are
consistent --- the model interpolates faithfully on authored data
but does not yet truly learn the underlying rule under multi-grid
synth --- and we believe both are necessary milestones for an
engine-modelling claim. The candidate fixes outlined above are
specific to the synth-ceiling gap.

\paragraph{Beyond PuzzleScript.}
The architecture is not domain-specific. Any deterministic
discrete-grid simulator with declarative rules (Conway's Game of
Life and its variants, cellular-automata gas dynamics, Asari-style
chemical-reaction grids) admits the same modeling pattern: tokenize
the rules, encode them via the slot encoder, train an NCA body
shared across iterations. We expect the comparison against
non-iterative baselines to favor the NCA more strongly as the
underlying engine's outer-loop iteration count grows.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our slot encoder is level-independent: the slots depend only on the
game's rule tokens, not on the level's spatial state. This is the
intended design (one encoded game $\Rightarrow$ many levels) but it
also caps what the slots can carry. A version where slots can attend
back into the input grid (perceiver-IO style) would be a natural
extension. We also restrict to single-step prediction; multi-step
training (predict $s_{t+k}$ for $k > 1$) is straightforward to add
and not yet explored.
