\section{Implementation details}
\label{app:impl}

\subsection{Model construction}

All models are implemented in JAX/Flax with the Adam optimizer. The
latent encoder is identical across architectures (see
\S\ref{sec:method}). The hidden grid dimension $d_h$, the cell
update structure, and the readout heads are also shared; only the
spatial-update body (rule-attention NCA, CNN, U-Net, or ViT) varies.

\subsection{Tokenization}

Each game's tokenized representation is a sequence of integers from
a $\approx 140$-symbol vocabulary covering: object names, layer
references, rule operators, action types, the 5$\times$5 sprite RGBA
values when sprite tokens are enabled, and per-rule kernel
separators. A single token sequence per game serves as the encoder
input.

\section{Per-game $\times$ per-architecture grid}
\label{app:per-game-arch}

To check how architecture choice interacts with game type, we train
one model per (game, architecture-bucket) cell on five games covering
distinct mechanic classes---%
\textit{Microban} (single-step Sokoban-style box pushing: PuzzleScript's
basic push rule applies force to the cell directly in front of the
player, so a row of two boxes is \emph{not} chained-pushed by a single
action),
\textit{Heroes of Sokoban} (multi-bracket character swap and
projectile travel),
\textit{Bouncers} (non-axis-aligned looping projectile),
\textit{neko puzzle} (restricted-orientation movement), and
\textit{Travelling salesman} (multi-bracket all-pairs)---against
four architecture buckets at $T{=}8$:
(A) pool ON, shared ($n_r{=}T$);
(B) pool ON, per-step ($n_r{=}1$);
(C) no-pool $+$ input-skip, shared;
(D) no-pool $+$ input-skip, per-step.
Each cell is trained on the game's authored level 0 only with the
canonical recipe (\S\ref{sec:experiments}); we evaluate BFS rollout
cell-error on the training level (L0) and on held-out levels
(L1$+$, mean) separately. Training budget is 15k updates per cell,
extended to 50k for \textit{Travelling salesman} after observing
visible loss descent at 15k. Single seed. The same grid on the
basic-Sokoban demo (a smaller variant of the same Sokoban dynamics
as \textit{Microban}) is consistent with the \textit{Microban} row
and would double-count the same mechanic in the figure.

\paragraph{Train-loss-vs-rollout-error decoupling, sharpened.}
\label{app:F3-sharpened}
A model with the lowest per-step train loss is not the model with the
lowest autoregressive rollout error. Concretely, in the over-capacity
regime we observe per-step models with $5\times$ lower train loss but
$3$--$4\times$ higher 50-step rollout error: small but systematic
per-step errors compound destructively under autoregression. The
per-game grid pushes the gap to its loudest form yet.
On \textit{Travelling salesman} at 50k updates all four architecture
buckets converge to within $1\%$ of the same train loss
($1.70\times 10^{-3}$ to $1.72\times 10^{-3}$), yet L0 BFS cell-error
splits $0\% / 0\% / 26.9\% / 45.1\%$ across buckets D / C / B / A.
Bucket~A's L0 rollout is in fact \emph{worse} at 50k than at 15k
($45.1\%$ vs.\ $24.0\%$) despite a $68\times$ lower train loss---%
additional optimization on a per-step minimum that compounds
destructively under autoregressive feedback. Buckets~C and D, which
replace global pool features with input-skip re-injection, converge to
the same train loss with a stable rollout. The pool-ON cells thus
appear to overfit per-step prediction in a way that does not survive
auto-regressive feedback.

\paragraph{Held-out gap and the synth-data prediction.}
Held-out cell-error (right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:per-game-arch-heatmap})
remains substantial across every cell ($7.3\%$--$56.8\%$). This is the
\emph{within-game} OOD-levels axis---a complementary, lesser form of
generalization than the OOD-rules / latent-sampling / inverse-fit
results in Appendix~\ref{app:ood}. Prior single-game work strongly
predicts that synthetic levels close most of this gap: training
synth-only on the basic-Sokoban demo ($w{=}7$) reached $0\%$ cell-error
on 155 held-out \textit{Microban} / \textit{Microban I} authored
levels, and multi-grid synth on \textit{neko puzzle} closed a
$3$--$8\%$ gap to $0.3\%$ once the synth widths included the authored
aspect ratio. Both signals suggest the right-panel gap here is data
scarcity, not an architectural ceiling.

\paragraph{Synth-only $\to$ all-authored generalization.}
\label{app:synth-only}
The within-game-generalization probe replaces each cell's authored
training set with $K{=}128$ synthetic levels generated at the
\emph{smallest-by-area} authored size of the target game and evaluates
on \emph{every} authored level (no L0 / heldout split: all authored
levels are out of distribution to the training set). We anchor on the
smallest authored size because (i) size-up generalization carries
synthetic small-grid training to bigger authored levels (Sokoban
$w{=}7 \to 0\%$ on 155 \textit{Microban}/\textit{Microban I}
authored levels in prior work) while size-down does not; (ii) using
a single size keeps the per-size data density at $K{=}128$, which
empirically matters: an early multi-grid variant
($K / n_{\text{sizes}}$ per size, $\approx 16$ levels per size for
\textit{Microban}) plateaued at $\approx 8\%$ BFS heldout while the same recipe
at fixed $K{=}128$ on a single small size reached $0.60\%$ BFS,
$0.02\%$ 1-step. We also tried an auto-bounding-box variant that
picks $\max W \times \max H$ for each game; it failed at $\approx 9\%$
BFS because many authored levels are much smaller than the bounding
box, and the synth distribution missed them. Smallest-by-area (rather
than per-axis $\min W \times \min H$) is used so the chosen size is
an actually-existing authored shape rather than a per-axis composite
(\textit{Microban}'s per-axis minimum is $6{\times}6$, but no authored
level is $6{\times}6$; smallest-by-area picks $6{\times}7$, which
appears in three authored levels).

The recipe trains on $K{=}128$ synthetic levels at the chosen size for
$30{,}000$ updates with the same four architecture buckets at $T{=}8$,
with synthetic-level generation in \emph{evolve} mode and a small
auxiliary token-decoder loss. To force the GA to spread the $K$-level
budget across the game's rule set rather than collapsing onto a single
easy mechanic, we enable rule-coverage-driven evolution: per-step
rule-firing telemetry biases the fitness to
$\text{BFS-iterations} + 100 \cdot |\text{unique rules fired}|$, and
at the end of evolution we greedily pick the $K$ levels that maximize
the \emph{union} of rules covered (rather than top-$K$ by individual
fitness). For small-rule-set games (e.g.\ Sokoban-class) this is a
no-op; for multi-bracket games (\textit{Heroes of Sokoban},
\textit{Travelling salesman}) it materially changes which $K$ levels enter
training. We also report the iterations-only baseline (no coverage
weighting, no union-greedy selection) as a side-by-side ablation
column in Table~\ref{tab:within-game-synth}. The pre-registered
prediction follows in \S\ref{app:within-game-prediction}. If pure
synth fails on a game we fall back to seeding from one or a handful
of authored levels alongside synth---a lesser form of within-game
generalization that still tests few-shot extrapolation.

\label{app:within-game}

\section{Architecture findings}
\label{app:arch-findings}

The architecture comparison in \S\ref{sec:results-baselines} reports a
single number per architecture; this appendix expands the underlying
architectural findings. We label the findings F1--F3 to align with
the architecture report released alongside the code.

\paragraph{(F1) Global pool is load-bearing; axis-cummax surprisingly
contributes even on Sokoban.}
A bare $3{\times}3$ convolution can only propagate one cell of context
per NCA step.
Several PuzzleScript rule patterns reference cells arbitrarily far apart along an axis or anywhere on the level (\texttt{[X | ... | Y]}, \texttt{[X][Y]}).
Without pool features the NCA needs $\Omega(\max(H,W))$ steps just to surface non-local information.
The per-feature ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation-pool} isolates which pool feature does the work: removing global-pool alone is as costly as removing all three ($+182\%$ relative BFS error).
Removing axis-cummax also costs $+48\%$ on \textsc{Sokoban-Evolved}, even though its target patterns---axis-aligned \texttt{[X | ... | Y]} matches---do not appear in Sokoban's rule set; seed variance on that row is large (1.30\% absolute), so the effect may be partly noise rather than a genuine inductive-bias contribution from prefix-max.
On a 19-game gallery preset and a controlled \textit{Collapse} no-pool ablation, turning pool off costs both convergence speed and final loss, with the no-pool model collapsing into the identity-prediction minimum even at $4{\times}$ the depth.

\paragraph{(F2) Shared NCA-body weights are the right inductive bias.}
The PuzzleScript engine has two natural levels of iteration: an
inner ordered list of rules applied once each per tick, and an outer
\texttt{again} loop that re-runs the rule list until the state stops
changing. We factor $T = n_\ell \cdot n_r$ accordingly. On a
single-game depth sweep ($T \in \{2, 4, 8, 16\}$), shared $T{=}8$
reaches $5.5\times$ lower train loss with $7\times$ fewer parameters
than the per-step body at the same depth; shared $T{=}2$ at
$14\times$ fewer parameters than the deepest per-step variant
matches it on oracle-action rollouts. Hierarchical $n_r > 1$ sits
between these extremes (\S\ref{app:hierarchy}).

\paragraph{(F3) Train loss decouples from autoregressive rollout error.}
\label{app:F3}
A model with the lowest train (per-step) loss is not the model with
the lowest autoregressive rollout error. Concretely, in the
over-capacity regime we observe per-step models with $5\times$ lower
train loss but $3$--$4\times$ higher 50-step rollout error. The
mechanism is small but systematic per-step errors compounding
destructively under autoregression. Reporting only best-loss hides
the rollout-drift cost of going deeper or wider.
Appendix~\ref{app:per-game-arch} reports a sharpened version of this
finding on a per-game $\times$ per-architecture grid: on
\textit{Travelling salesman} all four buckets converge to within
$1\%$ of the same train loss but split
$0\% / 0\% / 26.9\% / 45.1\%$ on L0 BFS cell-error.

\section{Limitations and future work: gated local updates}
\label{app:gated-updates}

Our NCA body applies an ungated convolutional update at every iteration: each step replaces the hidden cell vector with the output of a small conv $+$ activation stack (with optional input-skip re-injection).
By contrast, the closest prior local-update grid-engine model~\cite{bamford2020neural} uses a 2D Neural GPU~\cite{kaiser2016neuralgpu}, whose core is a convolutional GRU (CGRU) with explicit update and reset gates: the new state is a convex combination $u \odot s_{i-1} + (1-u) \odot c_i$ of the previous state and a candidate update.
Two concrete directions are worth exploring.
First, plugging a CGRU-style gate into our shared-body NCA would let the model express ``hold this cell unchanged'' as a learnable, content-dependent identity rather than relying on the residual recurrence we currently use; this is the same mechanism that lets a Neural GPU run for many iterations past convergence without drifting, and it interacts naturally with our adaptive-halting experiments (Appendix~\ref{app:halting}), where convergence-ST already exploits self-arrest at inference time.
Second, gating could change the train-loss-vs-rollout-error decoupling we report in F3 (Appendix~\ref{app:F3}): per-step models that overfit per-step prediction at the cost of autoregressive stability might be exactly the regime where a learned ``do nothing'' gate dampens compounding error.
Whether the inductive bias matches the PuzzleScript engine's outer-loop semantics as well as our ungated body does is an open empirical question---the engine's \texttt{again} loop also expresses ``re-run rules until the state stops changing,'' so a gating mechanism that learns a content-dependent halt is in principle a closer match to the target dynamics than our fixed-depth body.
We leave a controlled CGRU-vs-ungated comparison at matched parameter count and matched depth to future work.

\section{Additional related work}
\label{app:related}

\paragraph{Adaptive computation.}
PonderNet~\cite{banino2021pondernet} learns a per-input halting
distribution over a recurrent module, with a KL prior over the number
of iterations. Adaptive Computation Time~\cite{graves2016act} and
Universal Transformers~\cite{dehghani2018universal} are predecessors.
Our adaptive-halt experiments
(Appendix~\ref{app:halting}) adapt PonderNet to NCA world models
with a shared body, so that ``halt at step $k$'' coherently asks the
same rule set to converge in $k$ iterations rather than selecting
between $k$ different models.

\section{Adaptive halting}
\label{app:halting}

We compare four halting modes and a fixed-depth baseline on a custom
$1$-rule \textsc{varislide} game in which a single \texttt{again}-driven
slide rule pushes the player up to $d \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16\}$
cells per action across grid widths $\{6, 8, 10, 12, 16\}$. Modes:
\emph{ponder} (PonderNet ponder loss with learned halt head + KL
prior), \emph{uniform} (per-step loss equally weighted across all
$T$ steps), \emph{argmax-ST} (straight-through estimator on
$\arg\max_k p_k$), and \emph{convergence-ST} (halt at inference when
consecutive predictions stop changing, no learned head).

% \papertodo{Add halting figure: per-step error trajectory under each
% mode plus per-instance halt distribution as a function of slide
% distance.}

Across these modes we observe two phenomena. First, on tasks where
the dynamics fit cleanly in $\approx 1$--$2$ NCA steps (small-grid
varislide, \textsc{collapse}), all halting modes converge near the
dynamics-floor within their effective depth and drift past it;
convergence-ST recovers near-oracle performance via inference-time
stopping. Second, on the multi-grid synthetic regime where
memorization is unavailable, \emph{none} of the halting modes
reliably learn the underlying rule (\S\ref{app:ceiling}); the halt
head at best degenerates to halt-at-$k{=}1$ + identity prediction.

\section{Multi-grid synthetic data ceiling}
\label{app:ceiling}

When we move from a single authored level to the multi-grid synthetic
data regime ($\{6,8,10,12,16\}$ widths $\times$ 64 levels each),
train change-error climbs by $1000\times$
($5 \times 10^{-5} \to 0.05$--$0.10$), confirming that the model can
no longer 1-shot-memorize per-state outcomes. The model attempts to
learn the rule but plateaus: 50k updates at $d_h{=}256$ ($5\times$
more updates, $2\times$ wider) does not move the needle. Multi-seed
verification at $T \in \{8, 16, 32, 64\}$ shows per-seed argmax
variance dwarfs the recipe signal and best-loss is essentially
identical across all configurations. We discuss candidate fixes in
\S\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Held-out latent geometry as we scale games}
\label{app:scaling-curve}

For each held-out game we encode its tokens through the trained
encoder, compute cosine distance to every training game's encoded
latent, and report the smallest of those distances---the distance
to the held-out game's \emph{nearest training-game neighbor} in
latent space. We use the flattened
$K \cdot d_{\text{latent}} = 1024$-dim latent vector, and the median
across the 21 of 30 held-outs whose tokens fit within the smallest
training preset's maximum sequence length.

We treat this as a \emph{diagnostic}, not a quality metric. A
distance of zero is collapse, not perfect transfer (the held-out
game has been mapped onto an existing training point). A large
distance is off-manifold, also bad. The number is also naturally
expected to shrink as the training preset grows---with more
games the chance that some training game lies near any given
held-out target rises mechanically, independent of representation
quality. Predictive OoD performance---per-cell error of the dynamics model
on the held-out set's authored-level transitions, against an
identity baseline---is the load-bearing claim for unseen-game
generalization; the geometry numbers below are reported only for
context.

\subsection{Joint symbolic-decoder objective is free for in-distribution fidelity}
\label{app:joint-decoder}

\paragraph{Default training setup.}
The main-body Conditioning interface paragraph
(\S\ref{sec:method}) writes the training loss as
$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{state}} + \alpha \mathcal{L}_{\text{win}}$.
The actual training objective adds a third
auxiliary term $\beta \mathcal{L}_{\text{tok}}$ whose role is purely
to shape the latent space: the encoded latents $z$ are fed to a causal
token decoder $\mathrm{Dec}_\psi$ that is trained under teacher
forcing to reconstruct the original token sequence (decoder
architecture and reconstruction / sampling results in
Appendix~\ref{app:autoencoder}). All conditional runs reported in
the main body train with $\beta = 1.0$ by default; we never observed
the auxiliary loss to harm world-model fidelity, and switching it on
is what makes the latent-space probes in
Appendices~\ref{app:autoencoder} and \ref{app:inverse-fit} possible
(the decoder is what turns a latent back into program text).

A natural concern when adding this auxiliary loss is whether it
costs anything on the world-model's primary job---predicting the
next state. Table~\ref{tab:fidelity} compares two checkpoints that
share architecture, training preset (\textsc{Train-94}: 94 deduped
Gallery games), and update count, and differ only in whether the
decoder loss is on. Per-cell rollout error ties within $0.5$pp on
every regime, so the in-distribution dynamics-fidelity claim is
robust to the auxiliary loss.

\subsection{Symbolic decoder: reconstruction and sampling}
\label{app:autoencoder}

\paragraph{Decoder architecture.}
$\mathrm{Dec}_\psi$ is a four-layer causal Transformer over the same
$\approx 140$-symbol vocabulary used by the encoder. Each token position
self-attends causally to prior positions and cross-attends to the
$K$ latents at every layer. We train under teacher forcing: the input
sequence is the right-shifted target tokens with a learned BOS, and
the per-position loss is masked cross-entropy against the next
target token. Object names and sprite-RGB tokens are vocab-extended
on a per-game basis; the decoder treats them as ordinary tokens and
the detokenizer assigns generic names $\mathrm{Obj}_i$ on round-trip
to .puzzlescript source. Validity checks (parser-loadable +
JS-engine-compilable) are reported as separate metrics below, since
syntactic well-formedness and engine acceptance both diverge from
exact-token reconstruction in the OOD-sample regime.

The joint encoder + NCA + latent-token decoder is trained on
\textsc{Train-94} for 150k updates with $\beta = 1.0$ on the decoder
loss. The decoder converges to per-token cross-entropy
$\approx 2 \times 10^{-9}$ within the first 5k updates and holds
there throughout, adding 1.18M parameters on top of the 9.89M
world-model parameters and roughly 20\% to per-step wall-clock.

\paragraph{Token reconstruction accuracy} (per-position match rate
between the decoder's argmax prediction and the target token under
teacher forcing on training games): perfect reconstruction on
$76/94$ training games and a median per-position match of $1.000$
(mean $0.906$, min $0.276$). The latent bottleneck retains the
information needed to recover the full symbolic specification on the
majority of the corpus; the imperfect mean is dominated by a small
tail of long, sprite-rich games that exhaust the decoder's
positional budget.

\paragraph{Engine-load success rate} (fraction of decoded token
sequences that, after detokenization to PuzzleScript source, parse
and compile via the JS engine): $15/25$ engine-loadable PuzzleScript
programs ($60\%$) from greedy autoregressive decoding from random
Gaussian-fit latents; $9/9$ engine-loadable programs along a 9-point
linear interpolation between two endpoint training games
(\textit{Ebony \& Ivory} $\rightarrow$ \textit{BROKEN Naive Extended
Bodies}; $100\%$). The generations contain plausible push rules of
the form
\verb"[ > Obj2 | Obj4 ] -> [ > Obj2 | > Obj4 ]",
\texttt{late}-prefixed rules, and per-object $5{\times}5$ pixel
sprites with palettes consistent across the legend, the collision
layers, and the rule body.

\subsection{Inverse-fitting unobserved games from transitions}
\label{app:inverse-fit}

Because the dynamics model is conditioned on a continuous game
representation, we can freeze the NCA parameters and infer an
unobserved game embedding from transition data. Concretely, we
freeze the world model and decoder $(\theta, \psi)$ and treat
$z \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d_{\text{latent}}}$ as the only free
variable: the encoder $\mathrm{Enc}_\phi$ is bypassed entirely and
the optimized $z$ enters the world model through a latent-override
path. We initialize $z$ from a small Gaussian around the empirical
training-latent mean and optimize the per-cell next-state BCE used
during training, summed over the held-out target's observed
transitions, for 2{,}000 Adam steps at $\text{lr} = 5 \times 10^{-3}$
with batch size 32. Targets are drawn from the deduped pool and
verified disjoint from training by name and by token-hash. After
fitting, we report the cosine distance from the recovered $z$ to
its nearest training-game latent---``nearest train'' in
Table~\ref{tab:inverse-fit} means the closest training-game latent in
the flattened-latent space, not a comparison against a held-out
reference encoding of the target.

The optimization drives BCE down two-to-three orders of magnitude on
every target, so the frozen NCA can fit OoD dynamics through the
latent variable. The reported BCE is on the same transition tape
the latent was optimized against, however, so it measures the
expressive capacity of $(\theta, \psi)$ plus the optimization
landscape, not held-out generalization; an honest upgrade would
hold out a fraction of the tape, fit on the rest, and report
test-tape BCE.

On two of three targets the recovered latent's nearest training-game
neighbor matches a plausible mechanical sibling:
\textit{Cat Adventure 2} (a push-puzzle) lands closest to
\textit{neko puzzle} (also a push-puzzle), and
\textit{Monophobic Multiban} (a multi-character chase mechanic)
lands closest to \textit{Run em' over} (also multi-character /
chase). We do not yet have a systematic enumeration of how often
the nearest neighbor is mechanically related across the full
held-out set; the two matches above are illustrative.
