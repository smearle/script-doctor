\subsection{Scaling rule diversity: in-distribution and out-of-distribution}
\label{sec:results-scaling}
\label{sec:results-encoder-ablation}

A single rule-conditional NCA trained over a wider corpus must serve
two ends. In-distribution it should continue to fit the games it sees
even as the per-game share of capacity drops; out-of-distribution it
should transfer to unseen rule sets at evaluation time. We measure
both on the same checkpoints by training the canonical recipe
(shared-weights NCA, $n_h{=}256$, 150k updates) on
\textsc{Train-14} $\subset$ \textsc{Train-59} $\subset$
\textsc{Train-199} and evaluating each against (i) the 14 games
shared across all three presets, and (ii) \textsc{Heldout-26}, the
subset of the 30-game stratified held-out set. We isolate the encoder's contribution by
training a matched unconditional variant (no encoded slots) at every
scale, and report against the trivial identity baseline
$\hat s_{t+1}{=}s_t$, which lower-bounds the per-step state-change
rate. Conditional and unconditional models tie in-distribution at
\textsc{Train-14} (Table~\ref{tab:indist-scaling}: $1.63 / 0.11$
vs.\ $1.98 / 0.08$ random-AR mean / median; within $0.4$pp on every
regime), with the encoder's ID benefit emerging only at larger
corpora.

In-distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:intersection-slope-id}), both models
degrade on the shared 14 games as the corpus widens---data dilution at
fixed capacity---but rule-conditioning slows that dilution by roughly
an order of magnitude. Unconditional 1-step (TF) mean jumps from
$0.087\%$ (\textsc{Train-14}) to $2.20\%$ (\textsc{Train-59}), $\approx
25\times$, while conditional grows only $\approx 5\times$ over the
same range and is roughly flat to \textsc{Train-199} ($0.27\% \to
0.37\%$ mean; $0.021\% \to 0.157\%$ median). At \textsc{Train-199} the
unconditional baseline is $1.88\% / 0.46\%$, still $\approx 5\times$
the conditional mean and $\approx 3\times$ the median; per-game swings
can be dramatic (\textit{neko puzzle} goes from $0.000\%$ to $24.4\%$
when the encoder is removed). The encoder amortises shared dynamics
across games at fixed capacity.

Out-of-distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:intersection-slope-ood};
Appendix~\ref{app:cond-vs-uncond}, Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}),
\textsc{Train-14} conditional fails on most heldout games (mean 1-step
(TF) $14.8\%$, $\approx 7\times$ identity's $2.23\%$); with $14\times$
more rule diversity, \textsc{Train-199} conditional drops to $2.30\% /
1.55\%$, matching identity's mean, beating its median, and winning on
$11/26$ heldout games at 1-step (TF). The encoder's clean OOD edge,
however, holds only at the smallest training scale: at \textsc{Train-14}
conditional beats unconditional ($14.8\% / 3.5\%$ vs.\ $19.6\% / 4.4\%$);
at \textsc{Train-59} the ordering already wobbles (uncond lower mean
$4.33\%$ vs.\ $5.73\%$, cond lower median $2.58\%$ vs.\ $2.95\%$ and
closer to identity on $20/26$ games); and at \textsc{Train-199}
unconditional has both the lower median and more wins vs.\ identity
($14$ vs.\ $11$). At broader corpora unconditional and conditional are
roughly tied on the held-out set; we return to what the unconditional
model is exploiting in \S\ref{sec:discussion}. On random-action AR
rollout, where errors compound over 30 steps, identity ($\approx 2\%$
mean) is a much stronger baseline: every model drifts to $7$--$27\%$ and
no current checkpoint beats identity over the full rollout on more than
a single game.

\subsection{Architecture baselines}
\label{sec:results-baselines}
\label{sec:results-multigame}

The preceding scaling experiment fixed a single architecture---a
shared-weights NCA with $n_h{=}256$.
We now ask whether that choice survives head-to-head against stronger
spatial bodies on three corpora that
probe different facets of the problem: \textsc{Train-14} (14 games,
$4{\times}4$ to $32{\times}24$ grids, rule counts 1--14) for
multi-game capacity sharing, \textsc{Sokoban-Authored} (10
\textit{Microban} levels) for the per-rule fitting limit (where slot
cross-attention can in principle memorize per-state outcomes), and
\textsc{Sokoban-Evolved} (widths $\{5,6,7,8\}^2$ $\times$ 256
evolved levels each; 1024 distinct levels share one rule set) where
that memorization crutch is removed. The per-step NCA uses
independent weights per iteration; CNN, U-Net, and ViT replace the
iterated local update with a non-iterative spatial body. All five
share the slot encoder and readout heads. Single-game runs train for
10k updates per seed, \textsc{Train-14} for 20k, $d_h=256$;
the per-step training objective does not separate architectures
(all five fit train cross-entropy within $\approx 2\%$ except ViT on
\textsc{Sokoban-Evolved}, best loss $0.084 \pm 0.002$ vs.\
$0.069 \pm 0.003$ for the rest).

Both NCA variants outperform all three non-NCA baselines on multi-game
\textsc{Train-14} BFS-AR (U-Net at $1.8\times$, ViT at $2.3\times$
NCA-perstep's error; the NCA/non-NCA gap is robust to seed). Within the
NCA family the per-step body has the lowest BFS-AR error ($7.05\% \pm
0.87\%$) and one-step error ($0.25\% \pm 0.04\%$), while the shared
body has $5.8\times$ lower seed-to-seed variance and $3.8\times$ better
parameter efficiency; at $\pm 1\sigma$ the two overlap on aggregate
BFS-AR. ViT is the only architecture failing to fit the per-step
objective (best loss $\approx 8 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\approx 0$),
confirming the structural mismatch (per-game breakdowns in
Appendix~\ref{app:per-game}).
On \textsc{Sokoban-Authored} levels the shared-weights NCA wins
outright on BFS-AR ($0.003\% \pm 0.004\%$) at the fewest parameters
(1.94M)---an order of magnitude below the same-depth CNN ($0.055\%$,
$2.6\times$ more params) and two orders below the per-step NCA
($0.283\%$, $3.8\times$ more params); U-Net's 2-level downsampling
loses fine-grained player position on small grids ($3.13\%$), and ViT
fails to fit the data ($9.32\%$ BFS-AR; one-step $1.49\%$ vs.\
$\approx 0$ for the rest).
Removing the per-state memorisation crutch (\textsc{Sokoban-Evolved},
1024 levels sharing one rule set) ties shared and per-step NCAs ($2.08\%$
vs.\ $2.09\%$ BFS-AR) but the shared variant gets there with
$2.8\times$ fewer parameters; CNN trails both at $2.82\%$ despite
roughly twice the depth. This is the only preset where any
architecture leaves an order-of-magnitude train-loss gap (ViT), which
shows up downstream as $\geq 4\times$ worse BFS-AR. Seed variance is
large ($\sigma > 1.5\%$ on BFS-AR for per-step NCA, CNN, and the
input-skip-disabled ablation row) but the gap to U-Net/ViT holds at
$\pm 1\sigma$. Longer-form architecture findings (per-game grid,
hierarchical weight sharing) are in Appendix~\ref{app:per-game-arch}.

Decomposing the shared-NCA recipe by ablating each pool flavour and
input-skip individually (Table~\ref{tab:ablation-pool}, $\Delta$BFS as
relative BFS-AR increase): pool features carry most of the non-local
handling---dropping all three ($+316\%$) costs roughly twice removing
\emph{global-pool} alone ($+182\%$), with \emph{axis-pool} next
($+123\%$) and \emph{axis-cummax} a smaller $+48\%$. The small
axis-cummax contribution is consistent with the push rule
\texttt{[ > Player | Crate ] -> [ > Player | > Crate ]} mostly using
its $3{\times}3$ neighbourhood; we expect a larger contribution on
games like \textit{neko puzzle}, whose rule
\texttt{[ > Player | ... | Fruit ] -> [ | ... | Player ]} reaches
across the row (multi-game version: future work).
\emph{Input-skip} raises BFS-AR by $+54\%$ when removed but
\emph{lowers} one-step (TF) error ($0.168\%$ vs.\ $0.274\%$):
re-injecting $(s_t, a_t)$ at every step anchors the body to its input,
trading per-step fit for AR robustness when the rollout feeds back
slightly-off predictions.
