\subsection{Architecture baselines}
\label{sec:results-baselines}
\label{sec:results-multigame}

We compare the shared-weights NCA against three non-iterative spatial
bodies (CNN, U-Net, ViT) and a per-step-weights NCA ablation on three
corpora that probe different facets of the problem:
\textsc{Sokoban-Authored} (10 \textit{Microban} levels) for the
per-rule fitting limit (where slot cross-attention can in principle
memorize per-state outcomes), \textsc{Sokoban-Evolved} (widths
$\{5,6,7,8\}^2$ $\times$ 256 evolved levels each; 1024 distinct levels
share one rule set) where that memorization crutch is removed, and
\textsc{Train-14} (14 games, $4{\times}4$ to $32{\times}24$ grids,
rule counts 1--14) for multi-game capacity sharing.
The per-step NCA uses independent weights per iteration; CNN, U-Net,
and ViT replace the iterated local update with a non-iterative spatial
body.
All five share the slot encoder and readout heads.
Single-game runs train for 10k updates per seed, \textsc{Train-14} for
20k, $d_h=256$; the per-step training objective does not separate
architectures (all five fit train cross-entropy within $\approx 2\%$
except ViT on \textsc{Sokoban-Evolved}, best loss $0.084 \pm 0.002$
vs.\ $0.069 \pm 0.003$ for the rest).

On \textsc{Sokoban-Authored} levels the shared-weights NCA wins
outright on BFS-AR ($0.003\% \pm 0.004\%$) at the fewest parameters
(1.94M)---an order of magnitude below the same-depth CNN ($0.055\%$,
$2.6\times$ more params) and two orders below the per-step NCA
($0.283\%$, $3.8\times$ more params); U-Net's 2-level downsampling
loses fine-grained player position on small grids ($3.13\%$), and ViT
fails to fit the data ($9.32\%$ BFS-AR; one-step $1.49\%$ vs.\
$\approx 0$ for the rest).
A single-grid win does not, however, establish learned dynamics, since
slot cross-attention can in principle memorize per-state outcomes; we
return to this caveat in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.

Removing the per-state memorization crutch (\textsc{Sokoban-Evolved},
1024 levels sharing one rule set) ties shared and per-step NCAs ($2.08\%$
vs.\ $2.09\%$ BFS-AR) but the shared variant gets there with
$2.8\times$ fewer parameters; CNN trails both at $2.82\%$ despite
roughly twice the depth.
This is the only preset where any architecture leaves an
order-of-magnitude train-loss gap (ViT), which shows up downstream as
$\geq 4\times$ worse BFS-AR.
Seed variance is large ($\sigma > 1.5\%$ on BFS-AR for per-step NCA,
CNN, and the input-skip-disabled ablation row) but the gap to U-Net /
ViT holds at $\pm 1\sigma$.

Decomposing the shared-NCA recipe by ablating each pool feature and
input-skip individually on \textsc{Sokoban-Evolved}
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation-pool}, $\Delta$BFS as relative BFS-AR
increase): pool features carry most of the non-local
handling---dropping all three ($+316\%$) costs roughly twice removing
\emph{global-pool} alone ($+182\%$), with \emph{axis-pool} next
($+123\%$) and \emph{axis-cummax} a smaller $+48\%$.
The axis-cummax contribution is surprising given that Sokoban's push rule
\texttt{[ > Player | Crate ] -> [ > Player | > Crate ]} only references its $3{\times}3$ neighborhood and should not need directional prefix-max at all; the seed std on the no-axis-cummax row is large (1.30\% absolute on a 3.07\% mean), so part of the gap is plausibly noise.
We expect a cleaner axis-cummax win on games like \textit{neko puzzle}, whose rule \texttt{[ > Player | ... | Fruit ] -> [ | ... | Player ]} reaches across the row (multi-game version: future work).
\emph{Input-skip} raises BFS-AR by $+54\%$ when removed but
\emph{lowers} one-step (TF) error ($0.168\%$ vs.\ $0.274\%$):
re-injecting $(s_t, a_t)$ at every step anchors the body to its input,
trading per-step fit for AR robustness when the rollout feeds back
slightly-off predictions.

Scaling up to multi-game \textsc{Train-14}, both NCA variants
outperform all three non-NCA baselines on BFS-AR (U-Net at $1.8\times$,
ViT at $2.3\times$ NCA-perstep's error; the NCA / non-NCA gap is
robust to seed).
Within the NCA family the per-step body has the lowest BFS-AR error
($7.05\% \pm 0.87\%$) and one-step error ($0.25\% \pm 0.04\%$), while
the shared body has $5.8\times$ lower seed-to-seed variance and
$3.8\times$ better parameter efficiency; at $\pm 1\sigma$ the two
overlap on aggregate BFS-AR.
ViT is the only architecture failing to fit the per-step objective
(best loss $\approx 8 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\approx 0$), confirming the
structural mismatch (per-game breakdowns in Appendix~\ref{app:per-game}).
Longer-form architecture findings (per-game grid, hierarchical weight
sharing) are in Appendix~\ref{app:per-game-arch}.

\subsection{Scaling rule diversity: in-distribution and out-of-distribution}
\label{sec:results-scaling}
\label{sec:results-encoder-ablation}

A single rule-conditional NCA trained over a wider corpus must serve
two ends. In-distribution it should continue to fit the games it sees
even as the per-game share of capacity drops; out-of-distribution it
should transfer to unseen rule sets at evaluation time. We measure
both on the same checkpoints by training the canonical recipe
(shared-weights NCA, $n_h{=}256$, 150k updates) on
\textsc{Train-14} $\subset$ \textsc{Train-59} $\subset$
\textsc{Train-199} and evaluating each against (i) the 14 games
shared across all three presets, and (ii) \textsc{Heldout-26}, the
subset of the 30-game stratified held-out set. We isolate the encoder's contribution by
training a matched unconditional variant (no encoded slots) at every
scale, and report against the trivial identity baseline
$\hat s_{t+1}{=}s_t$, which lower-bounds the per-step state-change
rate. Conditional and unconditional models tie in-distribution at
\textsc{Train-14} (Table~\ref{tab:indist-scaling}: $1.63 / 0.11$
vs.\ $1.98 / 0.08$ random-AR mean / median; within $0.4$pp on every
regime), with the encoder's ID benefit emerging only at larger
corpora.

In-distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:intersection-slope-id}), both models
degrade on the shared 14 games as the corpus widens---data dilution at
fixed capacity---but rule-conditioning slows that dilution by roughly
an order of magnitude. Unconditional 1-step (TF) mean jumps from
$0.087\%$ (\textsc{Train-14}) to $2.20\%$ (\textsc{Train-59}), $\approx
25\times$, while conditional grows only $\approx 5\times$ over the
same range and is roughly flat to \textsc{Train-199} ($0.27\% \to
0.37\%$ mean; $0.021\% \to 0.157\%$ median). At \textsc{Train-199} the
unconditional baseline is $1.88\% / 0.46\%$, still $\approx 5\times$
the conditional mean and $\approx 3\times$ the median; per-game swings
can be dramatic (\textit{neko puzzle} goes from $0.000\%$ to $24.4\%$
when the encoder is removed; per-game cells in
Figure~\ref{fig:intersection-heatmap}). The encoder amortizes shared
dynamics across games at fixed capacity.

Out-of-distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:intersection-slope-ood};
Appendix~\ref{app:cond-vs-uncond}, Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}),
\textsc{Train-14} conditional fails on most heldout games (mean 1-step
(TF) $14.8\%$, $\approx 7\times$ identity's $2.13\%$); with $14\times$
more rule diversity, \textsc{Train-199} conditional drops to $2.44\% /
2.00\%$, matching identity's mean, beating its median, and winning on
$13/26$ heldout games at 1-step (TF). Conditional and unconditional
are roughly tied OOD at every training scale, with the per-metric
ordering shifting between corpora and within seed variation: at
\textsc{Train-14} (uncond averaged over 3 seeds, cond 1 seed) cond
mean $14.8\%$ vs.\ uncond $16.9\%$ but with near-identical medians
($3.45\%$ vs.\ $3.53\%$) and uncond winning on more games ($8$ vs.\
$7$); at \textsc{Train-59} the means roughly match ($4.69\%$ vs.\
$4.52\%$); at \textsc{Train-199} uncond has the lower median ($1.52\%$
vs.\ $2.00\%$) and one more win vs.\ identity ($14$ vs.\ $13$).
The per-game scatter (Figure~\ref{fig:ood-per-game-scatter}) shows
that cond and uncond beat identity on largely disjoint subsets at
\textsc{Train-199}, so the encoder is not a strict superset of the
unconditional baseline on this set; we return to what the
unconditional model is exploiting in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
On random-action AR rollout
(Appendix~\ref{app:rollout-ar}), where errors compound over 30 steps,
identity ($\approx 2\%$ mean) is a much stronger baseline: every model
drifts to $7$--$27\%$ and no current checkpoint beats identity over
the full rollout on more than a single game.
