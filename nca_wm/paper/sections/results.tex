\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{NCA architecture for per-game world models}
\label{sec:results-baselines}

We first fix a single rule family (Sokoban~\cite{sokoban}) and ask which architecture
best predicts per-step dynamics. Two scopes: (i) 10 authored Sokoban
levels, where the model can in principle memorize per-state outcomes
via cross-attention over slot embeddings, and (ii) a multi-grid
synthetic regime (four widths $\{5,6,7,8\}^2$ $\times$ 256 levels per
width, sharing one Sokoban rule set) where per-state
memorization is unavailable (1024 distinct levels share one rule) and
the model has to learn the slide rule across grid sizes. We then
ablate the NCA's pool stack and input-skip on the multi-grid synth
preset to identify which features carry the architecture's advantage.

All runs are trained for 10k updates per seed. We report best train
cross-entropy, end-of-rollout cell-error rate under three policies
(random, BFS-optimal, A*-optimal), and final parameter count.

\paragraph{Single-game (Sokoban).}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines-sokoban} reports the head-to-head
comparison on 10 authored Sokoban levels.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Baselines vs.\ rule-conditioned NCA on Sokoban
    (10 authored levels).} Mean $\pm$ std over 3 seeds
    at 10k updates. Lower is better for loss and cell-error; A* first-
    divergence step is higher-is-better. Param count in millions;
    architectures sorted by BFS final cell-error.}
  \label{tab:baselines-sokoban}
  \small
  \input{figures/baselines/sokoban_authored}
\end{table}

The NCA with shared weights wins on BFS rollout
($0.11\% \pm 0.16\%$) at the fewest parameters (1.94M),
$2.9\times$ better than the same-depth feed-forward CNN
($0.32\% \pm 0.24\%$) at $2.6\times$ fewer params and
$4.3\times$ better than the per-step NCA ($0.47\% \pm 0.66\%$).
U-Net's 2-level downsampling loses fine-grained player position on
small grids; ViT's global self-attention diverges before the first
rollout step (mean first-divergence step under $1$). Best train loss
is essentially identical ($0.107 \pm 0.002$) across all five
architectures despite the $\geq 100\times$ swing in autoregressive
rollout error; we report both metrics throughout because the per-step
training objective is invisible to compounding rollout drift.

\paragraph{Multi-grid synthetic (memorization-free).}
To force the model to actually learn the rule,
Table~\ref{tab:baselines-synth} repeats the comparison on the
multi-grid synthetic regime.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Baselines vs.\ rule-conditioned NCA on Sokoban
    (multi-grid synthetic).} Mean $\pm$ std
    over 3 seeds at 10k updates; widths
    $\{5{\times}5, 6{\times}6, 7{\times}7, 8{\times}8\}$ with 256
    synthetic levels per width.}
  \label{tab:baselines-synth}
  \small
  \input{figures/baselines/sokoban_synth}

The shared-weights NCA again wins on BFS rollout
($4.37\% \pm 2.02\%$) at the fewest parameters, with the per-step
variant $1.4\times$ behind despite $2.8\times$ more parameters. ViT
fails to fit the train loss
($0.084 \pm 0.002$ vs.\ $0.069 \pm 0.003$ for the rest), confirming
the structural mismatch we saw in the single-game setting:
unrestricted global attention does not fit grid-rule dynamics well.
The seed-to-seed variance on this task is large
($\sigma > 2\%$ on BFS error for all NCA / CNN variants) but the
gap to U-Net and ViT is robust to it. Longer-form architecture
findings (per-game grid, hierarchical weight sharing) are in
Appendix~\ref{app:per-game-arch}.

\paragraph{Per-feature pool and input-skip ablation.}
We decompose the NCA-shared advantage on the multi-grid synth setting
(Table~\ref{tab:baselines-synth}) by individually disabling each
component of the pool stack and the input-skip connection. Every
NCA step in the full recipe sees three flavours of broadcast
non-local features---axis max-pool (row + column max), axis
cumulative max (directional prefix-max in each of four directions),
and grid-wide global max---plus a re-injection of the embedded
$(s_t, a_t)$ at every step.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Per-feature ablation of the NCA-shared recipe on
    multi-grid synth Sokoban.} Mean $\pm$ std over 3
    seeds at 10k updates. $\Delta$BFS reports the absolute increase
    in BFS final cell-error vs.\ the full recipe; relative figures
    in parentheses. Train loss is essentially constant across all
    configurations.}
  \label{tab:ablation-pool}
  \small
  \resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{%
  \input{figures/baselines/ablation_pool}%
  }
\end{table}

\emph{Global-pool} is the single most important pool feature: removing
it alone is as bad as removing all three pool flavours combined
($+145\%$ relative BFS error in both cases), indicating that
grid-wide presence-of-X signals are doing all the non-local work on
this task. \emph{Axis-cummax}---the directional prefix-max designed
for axis-aligned ``rule fires once X is in the same row/column as
Y'' patterns---contributes nothing measurable on
Sokoban: the push rule
\texttt{[ > Player | Crate ] -> [ > Player | > Crate ]} depends only
on the immediate $3{\times}3$ neighbourhood, not on a directional
axial lookup. We expect the cummax feature to recover its
load-bearing role on games whose rule sets do reference far-apart
same-axis cells (e.g.\ \textsc{collapse}, \textsc{varislide}); a
multi-game version of this ablation on \textsc{Train-14} is left to
future work. \emph{Input-skip} contributes a modest $+36\%$ but with
the highest variance across configurations ($\sigma = 4.24\%$),
suggesting it is a stability mechanism rather than an
information-carrying feature.

The architecture-axis findings here are derived from a single rule
family. We turn next to whether the shared-weights NCA continues to
win when the model must master many rule sets simultaneously, which
also serves as a partial robustness check on the architecture
ranking.

\subsection{Scaling to multiple games (in-distribution)}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

We extend the head-to-head architecture comparison to multi-game
training, then sweep the training corpus from 14 to 199 games under
the canonical conditional recipe to characterize how in-distribution
fidelity scales with the number of jointly trained games.

\paragraph{\textsc{Train-14} multi-game baselines.}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines-scaling14} extends the head-to-head to the
\textsc{Train-14} preset: 14 PuzzleScript games sharing the rule-slot
encoder, with grid sizes from $4{\times}4$ to $32{\times}24$ and rule
counts from a handful (Sokoban) to $\approx 30$
(\textit{Heroes of Sokoban}, \textit{Sokoboros}). 20k updates per
seed, $d_h = 256$.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Multi-game baselines on \textsc{Train-14}.}
    Mean $\pm$ std over 3 seeds at 20k updates. Cell-error rate
    averaged across games and levels. Both NCA variants beat all
    three non-NCA baselines; within the NCA family, the per-step
    body wins on BFS rollout while the shared body wins on train loss
    (parameter-efficient) and has notably lower seed-to-seed variance.}
  \label{tab:baselines-scaling14}
  \small
  \input{figures/baselines/scaling_14}
\end{table}

Both NCA variants outperform all three non-NCA baselines on
autoregressive rollout, with U-Net at $1.6\times$ and ViT at
$\approx 1.9\times$ NCA-perstep's BFS error; the NCA / non-NCA gap is
robust to seed (no overlap of $\pm 1\sigma$ intervals). Within the
NCA family the per-step body has the lowest mean BFS error
($12.39\% \pm 1.82\%$) while the shared body has $7.6\times$ lower
seed-to-seed variance and $3.8\times$ better parameter efficiency;
at $\pm 1\sigma$ the two NCA variants overlap on aggregate BFS
rollout, so we cannot distinguish them on this metric. ViT's train
loss is $\approx 10^{4}$ times higher than the NCA / CNN family
($\approx 8 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\approx 0$), confirming the structural
mismatch from the single-game setting. Per-game breakdowns are in
Appendix~\ref{app:per-game}.

Rule-conditioning lets a single set of weights master many games at
once. Table~\ref{tab:indist-scaling} reports per-cell rollout error

same three parameter-matched checkpoints whose held-out numbers
anchor Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}. Each cell is averaged
across games and levels under one of four rollout regimes: random
actions (autoregressive over 50 steps), random-action teacher-forced
(one step at a time), BFS-optimal oracle actions, and A*-optimal
oracle actions.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{In-distribution per-cell rollout error as the
    training corpus scales.} Mean / median across all training games
    (each game's number is the per-rollout-step mean across all
    authored levels). The three rows differ only in the training
    preset.}
  \label{tab:indist-scaling}
  \small
  \input{figures/indist_scaling/table}
\end{table}

The median game's 1-step teacher-forced cell-error is $0.01\%$ at
\textsc{Train-14}, $0.10\%$ at \textsc{Train-59}, and $0.45\%$ at
\textsc{Train-199}: even at the broadest preset a single
rule-conditioned NCA, fed only the tokenized rule text and the
current state, predicts the next state of the median training game
at $\geq 99.55\%$ per-cell accuracy. Multi-step rollout error climbs
to $\approx 10\%$ mean / $\approx 4.5\%$ median on \textsc{Train-199},
with mean error rising as the corpus widens; the mean is pulled up
by a tail of persistently-hard games whose state spaces are large or
whose rule structures involve cascading \texttt{again} loops, which
we discuss in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Effect of scaling on out-of-distribution performance}
\label{sec:results-encoder-ablation}

The in-distribution results above all rely on the rule-slot encoder.
We now isolate two questions about the encoder: (i) is it doing real
work in-distribution, or merely inflating parameter count? and (ii)
does its output transfer to \emph{unseen} games, and if so, how does
that depend on the diversity of the training corpus?

To answer (i) we compare a parameter-matched unconditional NCA
against the rule-conditional NCA on \textsc{Train-14}. The
unconditional model receives only the current state and action; the
rule-conditional model adds the encoded rule slots as cross-attention
context. Parameter counts are matched within $2\%$ ($n_h{=}448$ for
unconditional vs.\ $n_h{=}256$ + slot encoder for conditional). To
answer (ii) we add a 199-game broad-corpus run (\textsc{Train-199})
and measure how the cond-vs-uncond gap and the cond-vs-identity gap
evolve with rule diversity. Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}
reports in-distribution training-game rollout error \emph{and}
out-of-distribution held-out cell-error on \textsc{Heldout-30}
(30 games disjoint by name and token hash), against the trivial
\emph{identity} baseline that predicts $\hat{s}_{t+1}{=}s_t$.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Rule-conditioning, parameter-matched.} ID rollout =
  mean per-game cell-error on training games, random-action autoregressive.
  OOD step-1 / AR-30 = mean cell-error on \textsc{Heldout-30}; the identity
  baseline lower-bounds it at the per-step state-change rate. OOD wins
  counts games where the model's step-1 cell-error beats identity.}
  \label{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}
  \small
  \input{figures/cond_vs_uncond_match/match_table}
\end{table}

\textbf{Encoder isolates a clean in-distribution signal.}
At Train-14 scale the rule-conditional model nearly triples the
unconditional model's per-cell accuracy ($1.6\%$ vs.\ $4.9\%$
cell-error), confirming that the encoder is doing real work and not
merely inflating parameter count. Per-game it beats the unconditional
model on $18 / 29$ held-out games. The encoder is at minimum learning
to disambiguate which rule set is in play.

\textbf{Out-of-distribution generalization is a function of rule
diversity, not the encoder alone.} On \textsc{Heldout-30} the Train-14
conditional model still fails catastrophically ($16.4\%$ step-1
cell-error, $7\times$ worse than identity), barely better than
unconditional ($19.6\%$). With $14\times$ more rule diversity the
\textsc{Train-199} conditional model drops to $4.8\%$ at step 1---%
within $2.5$pp of the identity baseline---and matches or beats
identity on $9 / 29$ held-out games. The headline rule-conditional
contribution to OOD generalization is \emph{driven primarily by
broad-corpus rule diversity} rather than by the encoder architecture
in isolation. We discuss the implications, including the fraction of
games on which even \textsc{Train-199} fails (e.g.\ pseudo-random
spawn games, deeply cascaded \texttt{again} rules), in
\S\ref{sec:discussion}.
