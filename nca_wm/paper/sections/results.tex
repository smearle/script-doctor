\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We lead with our headline result---scaling rule diversity in a single
rule-conditional NCA improves both in-distribution fidelity and
transfer to unseen games (\S\ref{sec:results-scaling})---then work
backwards through the architectural choices that make it possible.
\S\ref{sec:results-multigame} fixes \textsc{Train-14} and asks which
spatial body wins under multi-game training, motivating the choice of
shared-weights NCA. \S\ref{sec:results-baselines} drills down to a
single rule family (Sokoban) to expose the same architectural
contrast in a memorization-free regime, and decomposes the NCA's pool
stack and input-skip contributions.

\subsection{Scaling rule diversity: in-distribution and out-of-distribution}
\label{sec:results-scaling}
\label{sec:results-encoder-ablation}

A single rule-conditional NCA trained over a wider corpus must serve
two ends. In-distribution it should continue to fit the games it sees
even as the per-game share of capacity drops; out-of-distribution it
should transfer to unseen rule sets at evaluation time. We measure
both on the same checkpoints by training the canonical recipe
(shared-weights NCA, $n_h{=}256$, 150k updates) on
\textsc{Train-14} $\subset$ \textsc{Train-59} $\subset$
\textsc{Train-199} and evaluating each against (i) the 14 games
shared across all three presets, and (ii) \textsc{Heldout-30}, a
30-game stratified set disjoint from every preset by filename and
token-sequence hash. We isolate the encoder's contribution by
training a matched unconditional variant (no encoded slots) at every
scale, and report against the trivial identity baseline
$\hat s_{t+1}{=}s_t$, which lower-bounds the per-step state-change
rate. Conditional and unconditional models tie in-distribution at
\textsc{Train-14} (Table~\ref{tab:indist-scaling}: $1.63 / 0.11$
vs.\ $1.98 / 0.08$ random-AR mean / median; within $0.4$pp on every
regime), with the encoder's ID benefit emerging only at larger
corpora.

\textbf{ID: rule-conditioning slows per-game dilution by an order of
magnitude.} (Figure~\ref{fig:intersection-slope}, left.)
Both conditional and unconditional models degrade on the shared 14
games as the corpus widens---data dilution at fixed capacity---but
rule-conditioning slows that dilution by roughly an order of
magnitude. The unconditional 1-step (TF) mean jumps from $0.087\%$
(\textsc{Train-14}) to $2.20\%$ (\textsc{Train-59}), $\approx
25\times$, while conditional grows only $\approx 5\times$ over the
same range and is roughly flat to \textsc{Train-199} ($0.27\% \to
0.37\%$ mean; $0.021\% \to 0.157\%$ median). At \textsc{Train-199}
the unconditional baseline is $1.88\%$ / $0.46\%$, still $\approx
5\times$ the conditional mean and $\approx 3\times$ the median.
Single games can swing dramatically: \textit{neko puzzle} goes from
$0.000\%$ to $24.4\%$ when the encoder is removed. The encoder
amortises shared dynamics across games at fixed capacity.

\textbf{OOD: encoder helps already at \textsc{Train-14}.}
(Figure~\ref{fig:intersection-slope}, right;
Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}.)
On \textsc{Heldout-30} the conditional model's 1-step (TF)
cell-error is $16.2\% / 3.7\%$ vs.\ unconditional $22.3\% / 5.2\%$
even at the smallest training scale---a $\approx 6$pp mean and
$\approx 1.5$pp median gap. Without an encoder the unconditional
model has nothing to disambiguate which rule set is in play and pays
for it on unseen rules even though it fits the training set fine.

\textbf{OOD: improves with rule diversity.}
The \textsc{Train-14} conditional model still fails on most heldout
games (mean 1-step (TF) $16.2\%$, $\approx 8\times$ identity's
$2.11\%$). With $14\times$ more rule diversity, \textsc{Train-199}
conditional drops to $2.91\% / 2.11\%$, matching identity's mean
within 1pp and beating identity on $11/29$ heldout games at 1-step
(TF). Aggregate per-preset cells
(Appendix~\ref{app:indist-scaling-aggregate}) confirm the trend.

\textbf{\textsc{Train-59} reverses on mean vs.\ median.}
The unconditional row's mean edges out the conditional row's
($4.65\%$ vs.\ $5.94\%$ 1-step (TF)) while the median reverses
($3.19\%$ vs.\ $2.94\%$), and the conditional model is closer to
identity on $18 / 29$ heldout games. The mean inversion is driven by
a few games where the conditional model produces wildly off
predictions ($39.1\%$ on one game vs.\ $2.0\%$ identity); the
unconditional baseline falls back to a near-identity predictor on
unfamiliar inputs (per-game error correlates $0.45$ with identity vs.\
$0.24$ for cond). The median is therefore the more informative
summary at this scale.

\textbf{Wins vs.\ identity (1-step (TF)).} The last column of
Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match} counts heldout games where the
model beats identity on 1-step (TF). Conditional wins step up at the
broad scale ($7 \to 6 \to 11$ across \textsc{Train-14},
\textsc{Train-59}, \textsc{Train-199}); the unconditional baseline
tracks within one game on the scales where it has been evaluated
($6$ and $7$). On random-action AR rollout, where errors compound
over 30 steps, identity ($\approx 2\%$ mean) is a much stronger
baseline: every model drifts to $9$--$30\%$ and no current checkpoint
beats identity over the full rollout on more than a single game.
Implications---including the games where even \textsc{Train-199}
fails (pseudo-random spawn, deeply cascaded \texttt{again})---are
in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Multi-game architecture baselines on \textsc{Train-14}}
\label{sec:results-multigame}

The headline result fixes a single architecture---a shared-weights
NCA with $n_h{=}256$. We now ask whether that choice survives a
head-to-head with stronger spatial bodies on the same multi-game
corpus.
Table~\ref{tab:baselines-scaling14} compares the shared-weights NCA
against a per-step NCA (independent weights per iteration) and three
non-iterative baselines (CNN, U-Net, ViT) on \textsc{Train-14}: 14
PuzzleScript games with grid sizes from $4{\times}4$ to
$32{\times}24$ and rule counts from one (basic Sokoban) to 14
(\textit{Kettle}). All five share the slot encoder and readout heads
and differ only in the spatial body. 20k updates per seed,
$d_h = 256$.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Multi-game baselines on \textsc{Train-14}.}
    Mean $\pm$ std over 3 seeds at 20k updates. Both error columns
    are per-cell, averaged across rollout steps and aggregated across
    games and levels. Both NCA variants beat all three non-NCA
    baselines; within the NCA family the per-step body has the lowest
    mean BFS-AR error, while the shared body has notably lower
    seed-to-seed variance and $3.8\times$ better parameter efficiency.}
  \label{tab:baselines-scaling14}
  \small
  \input{figures/baselines/scaling_14}
\end{table}

Both NCA variants outperform all three non-NCA baselines on
autoregressive rollout (U-Net at $1.8\times$, ViT at $2.3\times$
NCA-perstep's BFS-AR error); the NCA/non-NCA gap is robust to seed.
Within the NCA family the per-step body has the lowest BFS-AR error
($7.05\% \pm 0.87\%$) and one-step error ($0.25\% \pm 0.04\%$), while
the shared body has $5.8\times$ lower seed-to-seed variance and
$3.8\times$ better parameter efficiency; at $\pm 1\sigma$ the two NCA
variants overlap on aggregate BFS-AR. ViT is again the only
architecture failing to fit the per-step objective (best loss
$\approx 8 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\approx 0$), confirming the
structural mismatch. Per-game breakdowns: Appendix~\ref{app:per-game}.

\subsection{Per-game NCA architecture and pool ablation}
\label{sec:results-baselines}

To expose the same architectural contrast in a memorization-free
regime---and decompose what the NCA recipe is doing internally---we
zoom in to a single rule family (Sokoban) under two scopes:
(i) 10 authored levels, where the model can in principle memorize
per-state outcomes via slot cross-attention; and (ii) a multi-grid
synthetic regime (widths $\{5,6,7,8\}^2$ $\times$ 256 levels each;
1024 distinct levels share one rule set) where memorization is
unavailable. We then ablate the NCA's pool stack and input-skip on
the multi-grid synth preset.

All runs train for 10k updates per seed; metrics are averaged over
50-step rollouts. We report \emph{one-step (TF) cell-error}
(teacher-forced, random actions) and \emph{BFS-AR cell-error}
(autoregressive under BFS-optimal actions). All five architectures
fit train cross-entropy within $\approx 2\%$ of each other on every
preset except ViT on multi-grid synth (best loss
$0.084 \pm 0.002$ vs.\ $0.069 \pm 0.003$); we omit the train-loss
column from the main tables since the per-step objective is not
sensitive enough to separate models.

Table~\ref{tab:baselines-sokoban} reports the head-to-head
comparison on 10 authored Sokoban levels.

The NCA with shared weights wins on BFS-AR cell-error
($0.003\% \pm 0.004\%$) at the fewest parameters (1.94M), an order
of magnitude below the same-depth feed-forward CNN
($0.055\% \pm 0.075\%$, $2.6\times$ more params) and roughly two
orders of magnitude below the per-step NCA ($0.283\% \pm 0.400\%$,
$3.8\times$ more params). U-Net's 2-level downsampling loses
fine-grained player position on small grids ($3.13\%$); ViT's global
self-attention is the only architecture that fails to fit the data
even on this single-game preset ($9.32\%$ BFS-AR; one-step $1.49\%$
vs.\ $\approx 0$ for the rest), confirming that unrestricted global
attention does not match grid-rule dynamics.

The shared-weights and per-step NCAs are statistically tied on this
task ($2.08\% \pm 0.47\%$ vs.\ $2.09\% \pm 1.75\%$ BFS-AR
cell-error), but the shared variant matches that performance with
$2.8\times$ fewer parameters; CNN trails both at $2.82\% \pm 1.97\%$
despite roughly twice the depth. ViT fails to fit the multi-grid
synth data on the per-step training objective itself (best loss
$0.084 \pm 0.002$ vs.\ $0.069 \pm 0.003$ for the rest, the only
preset where any architecture leaves an order-of-magnitude gap on
train loss), and that gap shows up downstream as a $\geq 4\times$
worse BFS-AR cell-error vs.\ the NCA family. Seed-to-seed variance on
this task is large ($\sigma > 1.5\%$ on BFS-AR for the per-step NCA,
CNN, and the input-skip-disabled ablation row) but the gap to U-Net
and ViT remains robust at $\pm 1\sigma$. Longer-form architecture
findings (per-game grid, hierarchical weight sharing) are in
Appendix~\ref{app:per-game-arch}.

Percentages below are relative BFS-AR increases over the full recipe
(cf.\ $\Delta$BFS in Table~\ref{tab:ablation-pool}). Pool features
account for most of the non-local-context handling: dropping all
three ($+316\%$) costs roughly twice as much as the single most
important flavour, \emph{global-pool} ($+182\%$). \emph{Axis-pool} is
next ($+123\%$); \emph{axis-cummax}, designed for axis-aligned
``rule fires once X is in the same row/column as Y'' patterns,
contributes a smaller $+48\%$, consistent with the push rule
\texttt{[ > Player | Crate ] -> [ > Player | > Crate ]} depending
mostly on the $3{\times}3$ neighbourhood. We expect axis-cummax to
carry more weight on games like \textit{neko puzzle}, whose rule
\texttt{[ > Player | ... | Fruit ] -> [ | ... | Player ]} pushes a
fruit anywhere along the player's row; a multi-game version of this
ablation is left to future work.
\emph{Input-skip} raises BFS-AR by $+54\%$ when removed but
\emph{lowers} the one-step (TF) error ($0.168\%$ vs.\ $0.274\%$).
Re-injecting $(s_t, a_t)$ at every step anchors the body to the
input; without that anchor it fits engine transitions tighter at the
cost of robustness, and the resulting per-step errors compound when
the rollout feeds them back as off-manifold inputs.
