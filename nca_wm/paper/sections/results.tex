\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{NCA architecture for per-game world models}
\label{sec:results-baselines}

We first fix a single rule family (Sokoban~\cite{sokoban}) and ask which architecture
best predicts per-step dynamics. Two scopes: (i) 10 authored Sokoban
levels, where the model can in principle memorize per-state outcomes
via cross-attention over slot embeddings, and (ii) a multi-grid
synthetic regime (four widths $\{5,6,7,8\}^2$ $\times$ 256 levels per
width, sharing one Sokoban rule set) where per-state
memorization is unavailable (1024 distinct levels share one rule) and
the model has to learn the slide rule across grid sizes. We then
ablate the NCA's pool stack and input-skip on the multi-grid synth
preset to identify which features carry the architecture's advantage.

All runs are trained for 10k updates per seed. We report two
per-cell error metrics, both averaged across the steps of a 50-step
rollout: \emph{one-step (TF) cell-error} (teacher-forced, random
actions; isolates per-step prediction error) and \emph{BFS-AR
cell-error} (autoregressive rollout under BFS-optimal actions). All
five architectures fit the training cross-entropy to within $\sim 2\%$
of each other on every preset except ViT on multi-grid synth (best
loss $0.084 \pm 0.002$ vs.\ $0.069 \pm 0.003$ for the rest), so we
omit the train-loss column from the main-body tables and rely on the
rollout metrics, which the per-step training objective is not
sensitive to.

\paragraph{Single-game (Sokoban).}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines-sokoban} reports the head-to-head
comparison on 10 authored Sokoban levels.

The NCA with shared weights wins on BFS-AR cell-error
($0.003\% \pm 0.004\%$) at the fewest parameters (1.94M), an order
of magnitude below the same-depth feed-forward CNN
($0.055\% \pm 0.075\%$, $2.6\times$ more params) and roughly two
orders of magnitude below the per-step NCA ($0.283\% \pm 0.400\%$,
$3.8\times$ more params). U-Net's 2-level downsampling loses
fine-grained player position on small grids ($3.13\%$); ViT's global
self-attention is the only architecture that fails to fit the data
even on this single-game preset ($9.32\%$ BFS-AR; one-step $1.49\%$
vs.\ $\approx 0$ for the rest), confirming that unrestricted global
attention does not match grid-rule dynamics.

The shared-weights and per-step NCAs are statistically tied on this
task ($2.08\% \pm 0.47\%$ vs.\ $2.09\% \pm 1.75\%$ BFS-AR
cell-error), but the shared variant matches that performance with
$2.8\times$ fewer parameters; CNN trails both at $2.82\% \pm 1.97\%$
despite roughly twice the depth. ViT fails to fit the multi-grid
synth data on the per-step training objective itself (best loss
$0.084 \pm 0.002$ vs.\ $0.069 \pm 0.003$ for the rest, the only
preset where any architecture leaves an order-of-magnitude gap on
train loss), and that gap shows up downstream as a $\geq 4\times$
worse BFS-AR cell-error vs.\ the NCA family. Seed-to-seed variance on
this task is large ($\sigma > 1.5\%$ on BFS-AR for the per-step NCA,
CNN, and the input-skip-disabled ablation row) but the gap to U-Net
and ViT remains robust at $\pm 1\sigma$. Longer-form architecture
findings (per-game grid, hierarchical weight sharing) are in
Appendix~\ref{app:per-game-arch}.

\paragraph{Per-feature pool and input-skip ablation.}
We decompose the NCA-shared advantage on the multi-grid synth setting
(Table~\ref{tab:baselines-synth}) by individually disabling each
component of the pool stack and the input-skip connection. Every
NCA step in the full recipe sees three flavours of broadcast
non-local features---axis max-pool (row + column max), axis
cumulative max (directional prefix-max in each of four directions),
and grid-wide global max---plus a re-injection of the embedded
$(s_t, a_t)$ at every step.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Per-feature ablation of the NCA-shared recipe on
    multi-grid synth Sokoban.} Mean $\pm$ std over 3
    seeds at 10k updates. $\Delta$BFS reports the absolute increase
    in BFS-AR cell-error vs.\ the full recipe (relative figures in
    parentheses); both error columns are per-cell, averaged across
    rollout steps.}
  \label{tab:ablation-pool}
  \small
  \resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{%
  \input{figures/baselines/ablation_pool}%
  }
\end{table}

The pool features collectively account for the bulk of the
architecture's non-local-context handling: removing all three at
once ($+316\%$ relative BFS-AR error) costs roughly twice as much as
removing the single most important pool flavour, \emph{global-pool}
($+182\%$). \emph{Axis-pool} contributes the next-largest
chunk ($+123\%$); \emph{axis-cummax}---the directional prefix-max
designed for axis-aligned ``rule fires once X is in the same
row/column as Y'' patterns---contributes a small but measurable
$+48\%$, consistent with the push rule
\texttt{[ > Player | Crate ] -> [ > Player | > Crate ]} depending
mostly on the immediate $3{\times}3$ neighbourhood and only weakly on
a directional axial lookup. We expect axis-cummax to carry more
weight on games whose rule sets do reference far-apart same-axis
cells (e.g.\ \textsc{collapse}, \textsc{varislide}); a multi-game
version of this ablation on \textsc{Train-14} is left to future work.
\emph{Input-skip} contributes $+54\%$ on BFS-AR but \emph{lowers} the
teacher-forced one-step error ($0.168\%$ without it vs.\ $0.274\%$
baseline). One reading these numbers suggest---though further
analysis would be needed to confirm---is that re-injecting
$(s_t, a_t)$ at every NCA step anchors the body to the input;
without that anchor it fits engine transitions tighter at the cost
of robustness to its own slightly-off predictions, which then
compound as the rollout feeds them back as off-manifold inputs.

The architecture-axis findings here are derived from a single rule
family. We turn next to whether the shared-weights NCA continues to
win when the model must master many rule sets simultaneously, which
also serves as a partial robustness check on the architecture
ranking.

\subsection{Scaling to multiple games (in-distribution)}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

We extend the head-to-head architecture comparison to multi-game
training, then sweep the training corpus from 14 to 199 games under
the canonical conditional recipe to characterize how in-distribution
fidelity scales with the number of jointly trained games.

\paragraph{\textsc{Train-14} multi-game baselines.}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines-scaling14} extends the head-to-head to the
\textsc{Train-14} preset: 14 PuzzleScript games sharing the rule-slot
encoder, with grid sizes from $4{\times}4$ to $32{\times}24$ and rule
counts from a handful (Sokoban) to $\approx 30$
(\textit{Heroes of Sokoban}, \textit{Sokoboros}). 20k updates per
seed, $d_h = 256$.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Multi-game baselines on \textsc{Train-14}.}
    Mean $\pm$ std over 3 seeds at 20k updates. Both error columns
    are per-cell, averaged across rollout steps and aggregated across
    games and levels. Both NCA variants beat all three non-NCA
    baselines; within the NCA family the per-step body has the lowest
    mean BFS-AR error, while the shared body has notably lower
    seed-to-seed variance and $3.8\times$ better parameter efficiency.}
  \label{tab:baselines-scaling14}
  \small
  \input{figures/baselines/scaling_14}
\end{table}

Both NCA variants outperform all three non-NCA baselines on
autoregressive rollout, with U-Net at $1.8\times$ and ViT at
$2.3\times$ NCA-perstep's BFS-AR cell-error; the NCA / non-NCA gap is
robust to seed (no overlap of $\pm 1\sigma$ intervals on BFS-AR or on
one-step error). Within the NCA family the per-step body has the
lowest mean BFS-AR error ($7.05\% \pm 0.87\%$) and the lowest
one-step error ($0.25\% \pm 0.04\%$), while the shared body has
$5.8\times$ lower seed-to-seed variance ($\pm 0.15\%$ vs.\ $\pm
0.87\%$ on BFS-AR) and $3.8\times$ better parameter efficiency; at
$\pm 1\sigma$ the two NCA variants overlap on aggregate BFS-AR error,
so we cannot reliably separate them on this metric. ViT is also the
only architecture that fails to fit the per-step training objective
(best loss $\approx 8 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\approx 0$ for the NCA /
CNN family), confirming the structural mismatch from the single-game
setting. Per-game breakdowns are in Appendix~\ref{app:per-game}.

\paragraph{In-distribution mastery as the corpus scales.}
Rule-conditioning lets a single set of weights master many games at
once. Table~\ref{tab:indist-scaling} pairs each rule-conditional
checkpoint (\textsc{Train-14}, \textsc{Train-59}, \textsc{Train-199};
the same checkpoints whose held-out numbers anchor
Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}) with the unconditional baseline
at the same preset. Each cell is averaged across games, levels, and
rollout steps under one of four regimes: \emph{random (AR)},
autoregressive over 50 steps with random actions; \emph{1-step (TF)},
teacher-forced one-step prediction with random actions
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}); and \emph{BFS} / \emph{A*} oracle-action
autoregressive rollouts.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{In-distribution per-cell rollout error as the
    training corpus scales, with the unconditional baseline at each
    scale.} Mean / median across all training games (each game's
    number is the per-rollout-step mean across all authored levels).
    Cells marked ``--'' are pending evaluation; the Train-14 and
    Train-199 unconditional runs are still in flight.}
  \label{tab:indist-scaling}

The conditional model's median 1-step (TF) cell-error is $0.01\%$
at \textsc{Train-14}, $0.10\%$ at \textsc{Train-59}, and $0.45\%$
at \textsc{Train-199}: even at the broadest preset a single
rule-conditioned NCA, fed only the tokenized rule text and the
current state, predicts the next state of the median training game
at $\geq 99.55\%$ per-cell accuracy. Multi-step rollout error climbs
to $\approx 10\%$ mean / $\approx 4.5\%$ median on \textsc{Train-199},
with mean error rising as the corpus widens; the mean is pulled up
by a tail of persistently-hard games whose state spaces are large or
whose rule structures involve cascading \texttt{again} loops, which
we discuss in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.

The cond-vs-uncond contrast in-distribution is modest at every
scale we have evaluated: differences of $\le 0.4$pp on $1$-step
(TF) at \textsc{Train-14} ($1.63 / 0.11$ vs.\ $1.98 / 0.08$
random-AR mean / median) and $\le 1.5$pp at \textsc{Train-59}.
Where the encoder pulls its weight is on unseen rule systems,
which we examine next in \S\ref{sec:results-encoder-ablation}.

\subsection{Effect of scaling on out-of-distribution performance}
\label{sec:results-encoder-ablation}

The in-distribution results above all rely on the rule-slot encoder.
We now isolate two questions about the encoder: (i) is it doing real
work in-distribution, or merely inflating parameter count? and (ii)
does its output transfer to \emph{unseen} games, and if so, how does
that depend on the diversity of the training corpus?

To answer (i) we compare an unconditional NCA against the
rule-conditional NCA on \textsc{Train-14}. The unconditional model
receives only the current state and action; the rule-conditional
model adds the encoded rule slots as cross-attention context. To
answer (ii) we add a 199-game broad-corpus run (\textsc{Train-199})
and measure how the cond-vs-uncond gap and the cond-vs-identity gap
evolve with rule diversity. Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}
reports per-cell rollout error on \textsc{Heldout-30}---30 games
disjoint from training by name and token hash---under the same
random-action regimes as Table~\ref{tab:indist-scaling}, against the
trivial \emph{identity} baseline that predicts
$\hat{s}_{t+1}{=}s_t$.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Out-of-distribution per-cell rollout error on
    \textsc{Heldout-30}.} Mean / median across 29 held-out games
    that the model produced rollouts for. The four error columns
    mirror Table~\ref{tab:indist-scaling}; the BFS / A* oracle
    columns are pending an oracle-action heldout eval pass. The
    final column counts games where the model's mean error over the
    30-step random-action rollout is lower than identity's; the
    identity baseline predicts $\hat{s}_{t+1}{=}s_t$ and lower-bounds
    the per-step state-change rate.}
  \label{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}
  \small
  \input{figures/cond_vs_uncond_match/match_table}
\end{table}

\textbf{Encoder helps OOD; in-distribution it is essentially free.}
At \textsc{Train-14} scale the rule-conditional and unconditional
models are tied in-distribution
(Table~\ref{tab:indist-scaling}: $1.63 / 0.11$ vs.\ $1.98 / 0.08$
random-AR mean / median; differences within $0.4$pp on every
regime). On \textsc{Heldout-30}, however, the encoder helps
meaningfully: the conditional model's 1-step (TF) cell-error is
$16.2\% / 3.7\%$ mean / median against the unconditional model's
$22.3\% / 5.2\%$, a $\approx 6$pp mean and $\approx 1.5$pp median
gap. The unconditional model has nothing to disambiguate which
rule set is in play and pays for it on unseen rule systems even
though it fits the training set fine.

\textbf{Out-of-distribution generalization improves with rule
diversity.} On \textsc{Heldout-30} the \textsc{Train-14} conditional
model still fails on most games (mean $1$-step (TF) $16.2\%$,
$\approx 8\times$ identity's $2.11\%$). With $14\times$ more rule
diversity the \textsc{Train-199} conditional model drops to $2.91\%$
mean / $2.11\%$ median, matching identity's mean to within $1$pp
and beating identity on $11/29$ heldout games at the $1$-step (TF)
metric (\S\ref{sec:experiments}).

\textbf{The \textsc{Train-59} rows reverse on mean vs.\ median.}
At \textsc{Train-59} the unconditional row's mean edges out the
conditional row's ($4.65\%$ vs.\ $5.94\%$ $1$-step (TF)), but on
the median the ordering reverses ($3.19\%$ vs.\ $2.94\%$), and the
conditional model is closer to identity on $18 / 29$ heldout games
(per-game; not the same as the wins-vs-identity column). The mean
inversion is driven by a small number of games where the
conditional model produces wildly off predictions (e.g.\ a single
heldout game where its $1$-step (TF) cell-error averages $39.1\%$
against identity's $2.0\%$ on that game). One reading is that the
rule encoder occasionally lands the dynamics body on an
off-manifold slot embedding for rule sets that look different from
anything in training, and the body fires rules incorrectly across
most cells; the unconditional baseline, having no encoder to
mislead, falls back to a near-identity predictor on unfamiliar
inputs (its per-game error correlates $0.45$ with the identity
baseline vs.\ $0.24$ for cond). The typical-game median is therefore
the more informative summary of encoder benefit at this scale; we
report both.

\textbf{Wins vs.\ identity counted on $1$-step (TF) cell-error.} The
last column of Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match} counts heldout
games on which the model's $1$-step (TF) cell-error---the same
quantity averaged in the column to its left---is lower than
identity's. Conditional wins are flat at small corpora and step up
at the broad \textsc{Train-199} scale ($7 \to 6 \to 11$ across
\textsc{Train-14}, \textsc{Train-59}, \textsc{Train-199}); the
unconditional baseline tracks within one game on the two scales
where it has been evaluated ($6$ and $7$). On the random-action AR
rollout, where errors compound over the full $30$ steps, identity
($\approx 2\%$ mean, the per-step state-change rate) is a much
stronger baseline: every model accumulates drift to $9$--$30\%$
mean and no current checkpoint averages below identity over the
full rollout on more than a single game. We discuss the
implications, including the fraction of games on which even
\textsc{Train-199} fails (e.g.\ pseudo-random spawn games, deeply
cascaded \texttt{again} rules), in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
