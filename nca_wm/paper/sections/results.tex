\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Architecture comparison: NCA vs.\ non-iterative baselines}
\label{sec:results-baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines-microban} reports the head-to-head
comparison on Sokoban (10 authored Microban levels), trained for 10k
updates per seed at $d_h = 256$ with the canonical recipe
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}). We measure best train cross-entropy loss,
end-of-rollout cell-error rate under three policies (random,
BFS-optimal, A*-optimal), and final parameter count.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Baselines vs.\ rule-conditioned NCA on Sokoban
    (10 authored Microban levels).} Mean $\pm$ std over 3 seeds
    at 10k updates. Lower is better for loss and cell-error; A* first-
    divergence step is higher-is-better. Param count in millions;
    architectures sorted by BFS final cell-error.}
  \label{tab:baselines-microban}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l c c c c}
    \toprule
    Architecture & params (M) & best loss & BFS final-err & A* first-div \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{NCA (shared, $T{=}4, n_r{=}T$)} & \textbf{1.94} & $0.107 \pm 0.002$ & $\mathbf{0.11\% \pm 0.16\%}$ & $64$ \\
    CNN (4-block ResNet)                    & 5.00          & $0.107 \pm 0.002$ & $0.32\% \pm 0.24\%$         & $89$ \\
    NCA (per-step, $T{=}4, n_r{=}1$)        & 7.36          & $0.107 \pm 0.002$ & $0.47\% \pm 0.66\%$         & $35$ \\
    U-Net (2 levels)                        & 6.07          & $0.108 \pm 0.002$ & $4.84\% \pm 1.03\%$         & $14$ \\
    ViT (4 layers)                          & 3.99          & $0.107 \pm 0.002$ & $10.96\% \pm 0.50\%$        & $<1$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The NCA with shared weights wins on BFS rollout
($0.11\% \pm 0.16\%$) at the fewest parameters (1.94M),
$2.9\times$ better than the same-depth feed-forward CNN
($0.32\% \pm 0.24\%$) at $2.6\times$ fewer params and
$4.3\times$ better than the per-step NCA ($0.47\% \pm 0.66\%$).
U-Net's 2-level downsampling loses fine-grained player position on
small grids; ViT's global self-attention diverges before the first
rollout step (mean first-divergence step under $1$). Best train loss
is essentially identical ($0.107 \pm 0.002$) across all five
architectures despite the $\geq 100\times$ swing in autoregressive
rollout error; we report both metrics throughout because the per-step
training objective is invisible to compounding rollout drift.

The single-game setting allows the model to memorize per-state
outcomes via cross-attention over slot embeddings. To force the model
to actually learn the rule, Table~\ref{tab:baselines-synth} repeats
the comparison on the multi-grid synthetic regime: four widths
$\{5,6,7,8\}^2$ $\times$ 256 synthetic levels per width, sharing one
\textsc{sokoban\_basic} rule set. Per-state memorization is
unavailable (1024 distinct levels share one rule); the model has to
learn the slide rule across grid sizes.

    $\{5{\times}5, 6{\times}6, 7{\times}7, 8{\times}8\}$ with 256
    synthetic levels per width.}
  \label{tab:baselines-synth}
  \small

    \toprule

    CNN (4-block ResNet)                    & 5.00          & $0.069 \pm 0.003$ & $5.56\% \pm 3.12\%$         & $7.0$ \\
    NCA (per-step, $T{=}4, n_r{=}1$)        & 8.49          & $0.070 \pm 0.003$ & $5.95\% \pm 3.50\%$         & $13.8$ \\
    U-Net (2 levels)                        & 6.07          & $0.071 \pm 0.003$ & $8.33\% \pm 0.97\%$         & $0.5$ \\

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

The shared-weights NCA again wins on BFS rollout
($4.37\% \pm 2.02\%$) at the fewest parameters, with the per-step
variant $1.4\times$ behind despite $2.8\times$ more parameters. ViT
fails to fit the train loss
($0.084 \pm 0.002$ vs.\ $0.069 \pm 0.003$ for the rest), confirming
the structural mismatch we saw in the single-game setting:
unrestricted global attention does not fit grid-rule dynamics well.
The seed-to-seed variance on this task is large
($\sigma > 2\%$ on BFS error for all NCA / CNN variants) but the
gap to U-Net and ViT is robust to it. We expand on the role of pool
features (and \emph{which} pool flavour does the work) in the next
subsection; longer-form architecture findings (per-game grid,
hierarchical weight sharing) are in
Appendix~\ref{app:per-game-arch}.

\subsection{Per-feature pool and input-skip ablation}
\label{sec:results-pool}

We decompose the NCA-shared advantage on the multi-grid synth setting
(Table~\ref{tab:baselines-synth}) by individually disabling each
component of the pool stack and the input-skip connection. Every
NCA step in the full recipe sees three flavours of broadcast
non-local features---axis max-pool (row + column max), axis
cumulative max (directional prefix-max in each of four directions),
and grid-wide global max---plus a re-injection of the embedded
$(s_t, a_t)$ at every step.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Per-feature ablation of the NCA-shared recipe on
    multi-grid synth \textsc{sokoban\_basic}.} Mean $\pm$ std over 3
    seeds at 10k updates. $\Delta$BFS reports the absolute increase
    in BFS final cell-error vs.\ the full recipe; relative figures
    in parentheses. Train loss is essentially constant across all
    configurations.}
  \label{tab:ablation-pool}
  \small
  \resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{%
  \begin{tabular}{l c c c c}
    \toprule
    Recipe & params (M) & best loss & BFS final-err & $\Delta$BFS vs.\ full \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Full (axis + cummax + global + input-skip)} & 3.07 & $0.098 \pm 0.004$ & $\mathbf{4.37\% \pm 2.02\%}$ & --- \\
    \midrule
    no axis-cummax    & 2.94 & $0.098 \pm 0.003$ & $4.37\% \pm 2.24\%$  & $+0.00$ ($+0\%$) \\
    no input-skip     & 2.48 & $0.098 \pm 0.004$ & $5.95\% \pm 4.24\%$  & $+1.58$ ($+36\%$) \\
    no axis-pool      & 2.94 & $0.100 \pm 0.004$ & $6.75\% \pm 0.56\%$  & $+2.38$ ($+55\%$) \\
    no global-pool    & 3.01 & $0.098 \pm 0.005$ & $10.71\% \pm 2.57\%$ & $+6.35$ ($+145\%$) \\
    no pool at all    & 2.68 & $0.100 \pm 0.005$ & $10.71\% \pm 3.89\%$ & $+6.35$ ($+145\%$) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  }
\end{table}

\emph{Global-pool} is the single most important pool feature: removing
it alone is as bad as removing all three pool flavours combined
($+145\%$ relative BFS error in both cases), indicating that
grid-wide presence-of-X signals are doing all the non-local work on
this task. \emph{Axis-cummax}---the directional prefix-max designed
for axis-aligned ``rule fires once X is in the same row/column as
Y'' patterns---contributes nothing measurable on
\textsc{sokoban\_basic}: the Sokoban push rule
\texttt{[ > Player | Crate ] -> [ > Player | > Crate ]} depends only
on the immediate $3{\times}3$ neighbourhood, not on a directional
axial lookup. We expect the cummax feature to recover its
load-bearing role on games whose rule sets do reference far-apart
same-axis cells (e.g.\ \textsc{collapse}, \textsc{varislide}).
\emph{Input-skip} contributes a modest $+36\%$ but with the highest
variance across configurations ($\sigma = 4.24\%$), suggesting it is
a stability mechanism rather than an information-carrying feature.

\subsection{Multi-game scaling}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines-scaling14} extends the head-to-head to the
\textsc{Train-14} preset: 14 PuzzleScript games sharing the rule-slot
encoder, with grid sizes from $4{\times}4$ to $32{\times}24$ and rule
counts from a handful (basic Sokoban) to $\approx 30$
(\texttt{Heroes\_of\_Sokoban}, \texttt{Sokoboros}). 20k updates per
seed, $d_h = 256$.

Both NCA variants outperform all three non-NCA baselines on
autoregressive rollout, with U-Net at $1.6\times$ and ViT at
$\approx 1.9\times$ NCA-perstep's BFS error; the NCA / non-NCA gap is
robust to seed (no overlap of $\pm 1\sigma$ intervals). Within the
NCA family the per-step body has the lowest mean BFS error
($12.39\% \pm 1.82\%$) while the shared body has $7.6\times$ lower
seed-to-seed variance and $3.8\times$ better parameter efficiency;
at $\pm 1\sigma$ the two NCA variants overlap on aggregate BFS
rollout, so we cannot distinguish them on this metric. ViT's train
loss is $\approx 10^{4}$ times higher than the NCA / CNN family
($\approx 8 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $\approx 0$), confirming the structural
mismatch from the single-game setting. Per-game breakdowns are in
Appendix~\ref{app:per-game}.

\subsection{Rule-conditioning vs.\ unconditional NCA}
\label{sec:results-encoder-ablation}

The Train-14 baselines above all share the rule-slot encoder. To
isolate the encoder's contribution we compare a parameter-matched
unconditional NCA against the rule-conditional NCA on the same
preset, holding the recipe (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) constant.
The unconditional model receives only the current state and action;
the rule-conditional model adds the encoded rule slots as cross-
attention context, plus a jointly-trained symbolic decoder
($\beta = 1.0$). Parameter counts are matched within $2\%$
($n_h{=}448$ for unconditional vs.\ $n_h{=}256$ + slot encoder for
conditional), and both train for 150k updates. To measure how this
gap evolves with rule diversity, we also include a 199-game
broad-corpus run (\textsc{Train-199}) under the same conditional
recipe.
Table~\ref{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match} reports
in-distribution training-game rollout error and out-of-distribution
held-out cell-error on \textsc{Heldout-30} (30 games disjoint by name
and token hash), against the trivial \emph{identity} baseline that
predicts $\hat{s}_{t+1}{=}s_t$.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Rule-conditioning, parameter-matched.} \textsc{Train-14}
  rows trained for 150k updates with the same recipe; \textsc{Train-199}
  is the broad-corpus run under the same conditional recipe. ID rollout =
  mean per-game cell-error on training games, random-action autoregressive.
  OOD step-1 / AR-30 = mean cell-error on \textsc{Heldout-30}; the identity
  baseline lower-bounds it at the per-step state-change rate. OOD wins
  counts games where the model's step-1 cell-error beats identity.}
  \label{tab:cond-vs-uncond-match}
  \small
  \input{figures/cond_vs_uncond_match/match_table}
\end{table}

\textbf{Encoder isolates a clean in-distribution signal.}
At Train-14 scale the rule-conditional model nearly triples the
unconditional model's per-cell accuracy ($1.6\%$ vs.\ $4.9\%$
cell-error), confirming that the encoder is doing real work and not
merely inflating parameter count. Per-game it beats the unconditional
model on $18 / 29$ held-out games. The encoder is at minimum learning
to disambiguate which rule set is in play.

\textbf{Out-of-distribution generalization is a function of rule
diversity, not the encoder alone.} On \textsc{Heldout-30} the Train-14
conditional model still fails catastrophically ($16.4\%$ step-1
cell-error, $7\times$ worse than identity), barely better than
unconditional ($19.6\%$). With $14\times$ more rule diversity the
\textsc{Train-199} conditional model drops to $4.8\%$ at step 1---%
within $2.5$pp of the identity baseline---and matches or beats
identity on $9 / 29$ held-out games. The headline rule-conditional
contribution to OOD generalization is \emph{driven primarily by
broad-corpus rule diversity} rather than by the encoder architecture
in isolation. We discuss the implications, including the fraction of
games on which even \textsc{Train-199} fails (e.g.\ pseudo-random
spawn games, deeply cascaded \texttt{again} rules), in
\S\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{In-distribution mastery on the broad corpus}
\label{sec:results-fidelity}

Rule-conditioning lets a single set of weights master many games at
once. Table~\ref{tab:fidelity} reports per-cell rollout error on the
94-game broad-corpus preset \textsc{Train-94}, averaged across all
94 games and all authored levels per game, under four rollout
regimes: random actions (autoregressive),
random-action teacher-forced (one step at a time), BFS-optimal
oracle actions, and A*-optimal oracle actions. We compare the
encoder-only run ($\beta = 0$) and the joint-decoder run
($\beta = 1.0$), where $\beta$ controls the weight of the symbolic
token-decoder loss in the training objective.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{\textbf{Per-cell rollout error on the 94-game broad-corpus
  training preset.} Mean and median across all 94 games (each game's
  number is the per-rollout-step mean across all authored levels,
  50 steps for autoregressive regimes / level-length for BFS / A*).
  Adding the joint decoder objective is essentially \emph{free} on
  rollout fidelity: every regime ties within 0.5pp.}
  \label{tab:fidelity}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l c c c c}
    \toprule
    Regime & v3 mean & v3 median & v3+dec mean & v3+dec median \\
    \midrule
    random (AR)        & $7.41\%$ & $2.46\%$ & $8.07\%$ & $2.74\%$ \\
    random\_tf (1-step)& $1.39\%$ & $\mathbf{0.15\%}$ & $1.38\%$ & $\mathbf{0.14\%}$ \\
    BFS (oracle)       & $7.00\%$ & $1.86\%$ & $7.59\%$ & $2.35\%$ \\
    A* (oracle)        & $5.30\%$ & $2.22\%$ & $5.42\%$ & $2.67\%$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The 1-step teacher-forced cell-error of $\approx 0.15\%$ across 94 games
quantifies on-distribution fidelity in a single number: a single
rule-conditioned NCA, fed only the tokenized rule text and the
current state, predicts the next state of any of 94 different
authored games at $\geq 99.85\%$ per-cell accuracy median.
Multi-step rollout error climbs to $\approx 5$--$8\%$ across regimes,
with the median game still under $3\%$ on every regime. The mean is
pulled up by a small set of persistently-hard games whose state
spaces are large or whose rule structures involve cascading
\texttt{again} loops; we discuss this distribution in
\S\ref{sec:discussion}. Adding the joint symbolic-decoder objective
($\beta = 1.0$) ties the encoder-only run within $0.5$pp on every
regime, so the dynamics-fidelity claim is robust to that auxiliary
loss.
